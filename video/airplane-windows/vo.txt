Airplane windows are never square. And that's not a design choice. Square windows once tore planes apart.
1954. The de Havilland Comet. The world's first jet airliner. Fast, quiet, the future of flying. Then, in just three months, two Comets broke apart in mid-air.
To find out why, engineers sank an entire fuselage into a giant water tank, and pumped it up and down, again and again, like thousands of flights.
Then it ripped open. Right at the corner of a square opening.
Here's why. Every flight, the cabin is pressurized, so the metal stretches like a balloon. Then it relaxes when you land. A smooth curve spreads that stress evenly. But a sharp corner focuses it into one tiny point. Flight after flight, invisible cracks grow, until the metal gives up.
It's called metal fatigue.
So engineers rounded every corner. And that's why the window next to your seat looks like this.
